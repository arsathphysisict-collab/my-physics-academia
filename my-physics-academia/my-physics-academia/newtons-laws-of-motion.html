Newton’s Laws of Motion

Understand force, inertia and motion through three fundamental laws.

Newton’s laws of motion explain how forces affect the motion of objects. They form the foundation of classical mechanics and help us analyse situations ranging from a book resting on a table to a vehicle accelerating along a road.

1. What is force?

A force is an interaction that can change an object’s state of motion or deform it. A force is a vector quantity: it has both magnitude and direction. The SI unit of force is the newton (N).

Forces may involve direct contact, such as a push by a hand, or act without physical contact, such as gravitational and electric forces. In motion problems, we consider the net force—the vector sum of all forces acting on the chosen object.

2. Newton’s first law: the law of inertia

Statement: An object remains at rest, or continues to move with constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a non-zero net external force.

This means an object does not need a continuing net force to keep moving at constant velocity. A net force is needed to change its velocity—that is, to produce acceleration.

What is inertia?

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of rest or uniform straight-line motion. Mass is a measure of an object’s inertia. For the same kind of change in motion, an object with greater mass is harder to accelerate.

Everyday example: When a bus starts suddenly, a standing passenger may appear to lean backwards. The feet move with the bus as contact with the floor accelerates them, while the upper body tends to retain its initial state of rest. This difference in motion produces the observed effect.

Equilibrium connection: If the net external force is zero, acceleration is zero. The object may be at rest or may move with constant velocity. These are both states of equilibrium in the translational sense.

3. Newton’s second law: force and acceleration

Statement: The net external force acting on an object is equal to the rate of change of its momentum and acts in the direction of that change.

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity:

p = mv

For constant mass, Newton’s second law becomes:

Fnet = ma

Here, Fnet is the net force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in metres per second squared. The equation is a vector relation, so the direction of acceleration is the direction of the net force.

How do we obtain F = ma?

For constant mass, momentum is p = mv. Newton’s second law gives:

Fnet = Δp / Δt = m(Δv / Δt) = ma

In the limit of an infinitesimally small time interval, the rate of change is written as a derivative. This familiar form assumes constant mass and uses acceleration a = dv/dt.

What does the equation tell us?

  • For a fixed mass, greater net force produces greater acceleration.
  • For a fixed net force, greater mass produces smaller acceleration.
  • If the net force is zero, acceleration is zero; velocity remains constant.

4. Newton’s third law: action and reaction

Statement: When one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on the first object.

These forces are called an action-reaction pair. They act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other in the free-body diagram of a single object.

Example: When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backwards. The ground exerts a forward frictional force on your foot. This forward force helps you move forward. The two forces are equal and opposite, but one acts on the ground and the other acts on you.

5. Free-body diagrams: a useful problem-solving tool

A free-body diagram (FBD) represents the forces acting on one selected object. It helps prevent forces on different objects from being mixed together.

  1. Choose the object you want to analyse and represent it as a simple dot or box.
  2. Draw and label each external force acting on that object.
  3. Choose convenient coordinate axes and resolve angled forces into components if needed.
  4. Add forces vectorially to find the net force, then apply Newton’s second law along each axis.

Common forces include weight (near Earth, approximately mg downward), normal contact force, tension and friction. Include a force only when the corresponding interaction acts on the selected object.

6. Solved examples

Example 1: Finding acceleration

A net horizontal force of 12 N acts on a 3 kg object. Find its acceleration.

Given: Fnet = 12 N, m = 3 kg.

Using Fnet = ma:

a = Fnet / m = 12 / 3 = 4 m s−2

Answer: The object accelerates at 4 m s−2 in the direction of the net force.

Example 2: Finding net force

A 5 kg trolley accelerates at 2 m s−2 along a straight track. Find the net force on it.

Given: m = 5 kg, a = 2 m s−2.

Fnet = ma = 5 × 2 = 10 N.

Answer: The net force is 10 N in the direction of the acceleration.

7. Common misconceptions

  • “A force is always needed to keep an object moving.” Not if its velocity is constant and the net force is zero. A force is needed to change velocity.
  • “Action and reaction cancel each other.” They act on different objects. Forces cancel in a force sum only when they act on the same chosen object and are opposite.
  • “Mass and weight are the same.” Mass measures inertia and is measured in kilograms. Weight is the gravitational force on an object and is measured in newtons.
  • “If an object is moving, the net force must be in its direction of motion.” The net force determines acceleration, not necessarily velocity. An object can move forward while slowing down under a backward net force.

8. Quick recap

  • First law: Zero net force means zero acceleration; velocity remains constant.
  • Second law: Net force equals rate of change of momentum; for constant mass, Fnet = ma.
  • Third law: Interaction forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction and act on different objects.
  • Inertia: Resistance to a change in motion; mass measures inertia.

9. Frequently asked questions

What is the SI unit of force?

The SI unit is the newton (N). One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m s−2.

Can an object move when the net force on it is zero?

Yes. It can continue moving with constant velocity in a straight line. Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity.

Why do action and reaction forces not cancel?

Because they act on two different objects. To analyse one object, include only the forces acting on that object in its free-body diagram.

When can we use F = ma?

Use the vector equation Fnet = ma for a body of constant mass in an inertial reference frame. For changing mass, use the momentum form of Newton’s second law with care.
